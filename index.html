Each battery cell adds 1.5 V. Dots inside the resistor show how hard it is for charge to pass.

Try this

  1. Keep R fixed and double V. The current doubles: I ∝ V.
  2. Keep V fixed and double R. The current halves: I ∝ 1/R.
  3. Set V = 9 V and R = 10 Ω. This is the largest current here, 900 mA.

The idea

Ohm's law says the current through a conductor is proportional to the potential difference across it, at constant temperature: V = IR. One ohm is the resistance that lets 1 A flow when 1 V is applied.

Try this

  1. Double the length. R doubles: a longer wire gives charge a longer obstacle course.
  2. Double the area. R halves: a thicker wire gives charge more lanes.
  3. Raise ρ. The material itself resists more, like nichrome compared with copper.

The idea

Resistance depends on length, cross-section and material: R = ρL/A. Resistivity ρ belongs to the material, not its shape. Copper is about 1.6 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, nichrome about 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m, which is why heater coils use nichrome.

Try this

  1. Switch from series to parallel at the same voltage. Each bulb gets the full voltage and glows brighter, and the source supplies four times the current.
  2. Choose AC. The electrons rock back and forth and the bulbs flicker as the voltage passes through zero.
  3. Open the switch. Current stops everywhere in the loop, but the electrons are still in the wire.

The idea

Series: R = R₁ + R₂, same current through each part. Parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂, same voltage across each branch. Homes are wired in parallel so each appliance gets the full 220 V. Indian mains is AC at 50 Hz, far too fast to see, so this one is slowed down.
